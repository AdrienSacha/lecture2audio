# Learning science behind the course material

This document collects the evidence that bears on the design of the written, interactive course material of lecture2audio (one self-contained HTML page per lecture, with exact definitions, concise explanations, worked examples, check questions and step-through visualizations). The target reader is a first-year student with no prior knowledge who studies alone.

Method. Every source below was located through a web search in this session, and its bibliographic data (authors, year, venue, DOI or URL) and headline numbers were checked against at least one record (publisher page, PubMed, ERIC, Semantic Scholar or an author copy). Numbers that I could only find in a secondary summary are marked "secondary". Where I did not verify a claim, the text says so. Effect sizes are standardized mean differences (d or Hedges g) unless stated otherwise; by convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large, but laboratory effects of this size usually shrink in field settings. Two general cautions apply to the whole document. First, most multimedia and cognitive load experiments are short laboratory studies with college students and tests given minutes after study. Second, a design principle that holds on average can reverse for a given learner, as the expertise reversal effect shows. The rules in section 8 are therefore defaults to be tested on real readers, not laws.

Confidence labels used in section 8. Strong: supported by several meta-analyses or large preregistered trials with consistent direction. Moderate: supported by meta-analyses with substantial heterogeneity, or by a consistent body of laboratory work with limited field evidence. Weak: derived by inference from theory or from a small or contested literature, or a design convention with no direct test.

## 1. Working memory and cognitive load theory

**Capacity.** Cowan (2001) argued that the capacity of the focus of attention is about three to five chunks, not Miller's "seven plus or minus two", which Cowan describes as a rough estimate. Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24(1), 87-114. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922. The exact number is debated (the article appeared with open peer commentary), and what counts as a chunk depends on the learner's prior knowledge. For a novice every new symbol, term or rule is a separate chunk, so the practical limit on new items per screen is small.

**Cognitive load theory (CLT).** CLT holds that learning new, complex material is limited by a small working memory, while long-term memory is effectively unlimited. Sweller (2010) reformulated the theory around element interactivity: intrinsic load comes from the number of elements that must be processed simultaneously because they interact (inherent to the content), and extraneous load comes from element interactivity created by how the material is presented. Germane load is then defined as working-memory resources devoted to the intrinsic load, not as a third independent source. Sweller, J. (2010). Element interactivity and intrinsic, extraneous, and germane cognitive load. Educational Psychology Review, 22(2), 123-138. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9128-5. Contested: de Jong (2010) lists conceptual and methodological problems of the theory, including that the three load types cannot be measured independently and that many load measures are subjective. de Jong, T. (2010). Cognitive load theory, educational research, and instructional design: some food for thought. Instructional Science, 38(2), 105-134. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-009-9110-0. The theory is best treated as a productive design heuristic that has predicted many experimental effects, not as a validated quantitative model. The design consequence that matters here is that intrinsic load can be managed only by sequencing and chunking, and extraneous load can be removed by layout.

**Split attention.** When text and diagram must be mentally integrated, learners spend capacity on integration. Integrated formats outperformed split formats in Chandler, P., & Sweller, J. (1991). Cognitive load theory and the format of instruction. Cognition and Instruction, 8(4), 293-332. https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Cognitive-Load-Theory-and-the-Format-of-Instruction-Chandler-Sweller/d91c273c6b0cf2c7de5cdf85ec0fedcde300d522. In the multimedia literature the same idea is the spatial contiguity principle (labels next to the thing labelled).

**Worked-example effect.** Studying worked solutions is better than solving the same problems unaided for novices. A meta-analysis restricted to mathematics found g = 0.48 across 55 studies from elementary to university level, with correct examples working better than incorrect ones, and (a surprise) self-explanation prompts moderating the effect negatively. Barbieri, C. A., Miller-Cotto, D., Clerjuste, S. N., & Chawla, K. (2023). A meta-analysis of the worked examples effect on mathematics performance. Educational Psychology Review, 35, article 11. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1. Caveat: the evidence is mostly in mathematics and similar well-structured domains; CS evidence is supportive but I did not verify a meta-analysis for it.

**Expertise reversal.** Instructional support that helps novices becomes redundant, and can hurt, for learners who already know the material. Kalyuga, S., Ayres, P., Chandler, P., & Sweller, J. (2003). The expertise reversal effect. Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 23-31. https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4. Caveat: many demonstrations use subjective load ratings. Consequence for this project: the audience is novices, so full scaffolding is the right default, but the page should let a returning reader hide scaffolds (see rules 20 and 21).

**Multimedia principles.** Mayer's principles (coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial and temporal contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, modality) are largely compatible with CLT. Median effect sizes depend on the edition of the handbook and have been revised downward in later work. In the second edition of the Cambridge Handbook, Mayer and Fiorella report coherence d = 0.86 (23 of 23 tests), and signaling d = 0.41 (24 of 28 tests); a summary of the third edition gives coherence 0.86, signaling 0.69, and a much smaller redundancy effect of 0.10 (secondary; from search summaries of the handbook chapter, not checked against the book). Mayer, R. E., & Fiorella, L. (2014/2022). Chapters in The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning. https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E. Independent syntheses:

- Noetel et al. (2022), an overview of 29 reviews (1,189 studies, 78,177 participants), found 11 design principles with significantly positive effects on learning, with the largest for captioning, temporal and spatial contiguity, and signaling. Effects were larger for complex material and for system-paced materials than for self-paced ones. Noetel, M., et al. (2022). Multimedia design for learning: An overview of reviews with meta-meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 92(3), 413-454. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329. The self-paced qualification matters because a web page is self-paced, so expect smaller effects than the laboratory headline numbers.
- Signaling: g = 0.53 for retention and 0.33 for transfer over 103 studies. Schneider, S., Beege, M., Nebel, S., & Rey, G. D. (2018). A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media. Educational Research Review, 23, 1-24. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001. Its abstract reports that prior knowledge did not moderate the effect, whereas another paper says it did, so this point is unsettled.
- Segmenting: for system-paced material, segmenting increased learning time, reduced load and improved retention and transfer. Rey, G. D., Beege, M., Nebel, S., Wirzberger, M., Schmitt, T. H., & Schneider, S. (2019). A meta-analysis of the segmenting effect. Educational Psychology Review, 31(2), 389-419. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4. I did not verify the pooled effect size.
- Coherence (removing seductive details, meaning interesting but irrelevant material): a significant effect, small to medium for retention and medium for transfer. Rey, G. D. (2012). A review of research and a meta-analysis of the seductive detail effect. Educational Research Review, 7(3), 216-237. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003. A later multilevel analysis reported a smaller negative effect of such details (g = -0.16; Springer, 2025, found in search results, not checked in full), so the effect is real but modest.
- Modality (narration instead of on-screen text next to graphics): d = 0.72 over 43 effects. Ginns, P. (2005). Meta-analysis of the modality effect. Learning and Instruction, 15(4), 313-331. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2005.07.001. A replication with 86 effects found d = 0.38, falling to 0.20 after correction for publication bias (Reinwein, J Psycholinguistic Research, found in search results; not verified in full). This is an example of a shrinking effect. It matters little here because the course material is text and the spoken output is a separate product, but it warns against taking original effect sizes at face value.
- Pre-training (learn names and characteristics of the key concepts before the main explanation): reported as supported in 7 of 7 experiments, median d = 0.92 (secondary, from a handbook chapter abstract). Original study: Mayer, R. E., Mathias, A., & Wetzell, K. (2002). Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 8(3), 147-154. https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.8.3.147. The number of tests is small and the authors are the proponents of the principle, so treat it as moderate.

A general, honest limit: much of the multimedia literature was produced by the proponents of the theory, with short retention intervals. The direction of the main principles is consistent across independent meta-analyses; the sizes are uncertain.

## 2. Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, feedback, pretesting

**Overview.** Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice as having high utility, and summarization, highlighting and rereading as low. Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4-58. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266. The review also warns that most evidence comes from the laboratory, and that benefit depends on learner motivation and skill. Bjork and Bjork's "desirable difficulties" frame these effects: conditions that slow initial performance can improve long-term retention and transfer, but only difficulties the learner can overcome are desirable. Bjork, E. L., & Bjork, R. A. (2011). Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way. In Psychology and the Real World (pp. 56-64). Worth. https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/robert-a-bjork-publications/.

**Testing effect.** Roediger and Karpicke (2006) showed that students who were tested recalled more after a week than those who restudied, although restudy was better on an immediate test, and the students who restudied felt more confident. Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249-255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x. Rowland's meta-analysis of 159 effects from 61 studies gave g = 0.50, larger with feedback, with more effortful retrieval and with more complex materials. Rowland, C. A. (2014). The effect of testing versus restudy on retention. Psychological Bulletin, 140(6), 1432-1463. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559. Caveat: Rowland excluded applied research. Transfer is weaker: d = 0.40 over 192 effects when the transfer question is related to the practised one, and no reliable benefit (d = 0.16, not significant) for untested material that was merely studied alongside. Pan, S. C., & Rickard, T. C. (2018). Transfer of test-enhanced learning. Psychological Bulletin, 144(7), 710-756. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151. So check questions must cover every idea we want remembered, and not only some of them. Karpicke and Blunt (2011) found retrieval practice beat concept mapping for later inference questions. Science, 331(6018), 772-775. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327. A published Comment criticised how concept mapping was implemented, so the strength of the comparison is contested.

**Spacing.** Cepeda et al. (2006) synthesised 839 assessments in 317 experiments and found that the optimal gap between study sessions grows with the delay until the final test. Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354-380. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354. This synthesis is of verbal recall tasks, so generalisation to conceptual material is an inference. For a static page this means that review questions about earlier lectures must be resurfaced later, not only placed at the end of the lecture where the topic was taught.

**Interleaving.** Brunmair and Richter (2019) found g = 0.42 overall; benefit was larger for visual material and mathematics, and depended on similarity between categories. Psychological Bulletin, 145(11), 1029-1052. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209. A preregistered cluster RCT with 787 seventh-graders reported a large delayed-test advantage for interleaved mathematics practice (the figure d = 0.83 was reported on an IES grant page, which I treat as secondary). Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., Hartwig, M. K., & Cheung, C.-N. (2020). Journal of Educational Psychology, 112(1), 40-52. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367. Interleaving suits discriminating between similar procedures; it should not be used when the student first meets a concept.

**Feedback.** Hattie and Timperley (2007) report a high average effect of feedback (0.79 over 12 meta-analyses, secondary), with the variance: feedback about the task and process helps more than praise. Review of Educational Research, 77(1), 81-112. https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487. Rowland also found that feedback strengthens the testing effect. A static page can supply feedback only through the answer and a worked explanation shown after the student commits to an answer.

**Pretesting.** Attempting a question before studying, even unsuccessfully, improved later performance in five experiments. Richland, L. E., Kornell, N., & Kao, L. S. (2009). Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 15(3), 243-257. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016496. Related but different, and contested: "productive failure" (solve a problem before instruction) showed a moderate benefit for conceptual understanding in a meta-analysis of 166 comparisons, but only when the design followed its principles, and it needs some prior knowledge. Sinha, T., & Kapur, M. (2021). Review of Educational Research, 91(5), 761-798. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105. For complete novices working alone, unguided problem solving before instruction is risky: a meta-analysis of 164 studies found unassisted discovery worse than explicit instruction (d = -0.38) and assisted discovery better than other methods (d = 0.30). Alfieri, L., Brooks, P. J., Aldrich, N. J., & Tenenbaum, H. R. (2011). Journal of Educational Psychology, 103(1), 1-18. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017. The safe use of pretesting is therefore a short, low-stakes question about an idea about to be taught, with the answer revealed straight after.

## 3. Animation versus static visuals

The summary is that animation helps only in specific conditions, and that earlier claims of a general advantage did not hold up.

- Tversky, Morrison and Betrancourt (2002) reviewed the literature and concluded that animation often failed to beat static graphics, that apparent advantages usually came from unequal content or added interactivity, and that animations are often too fast or too complex to be perceived accurately. International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 57(4), 247-262. https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.2002.1017.
- Hegarty (2004) argued that learners must hold earlier frames in memory and integrate them with later ones, which loads working memory, and that the interesting questions concern when interactive dynamic displays are actually used well. Learning and Instruction, 14, 343-351. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2004.06.007 (I could confirm the citation; I read only secondary descriptions of the content).
- Höffler and Leutner (2007): 26 studies, 76 comparisons, mean d = 0.37 in favour of animation; larger for representational (not decorative) animation and for procedural-motor knowledge. Learning and Instruction, 17(6), 722-738. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2007.09.013.
- Berney and Bétrancourt (2016): 140 comparisons, g = 0.226 overall; larger when system-paced (0.309) or with spoken commentary (0.336), and when there was no accompanying text (0.883), and better for iconic visualisations. Computers & Education, 101, 150-167. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2016.06.005. A later analysis found animation clearly better when the specifics of change over time must be learned (Ploetzner, Berney & Bétrancourt, 2020, J Computer Assisted Learning; found in results, not read).

Hegarty's and Tversky's recurring recommendations (as I understand them from the abstracts I verified; the exact wording was not checked in the full text) are that dynamic displays should be slow enough to perceive, segmented into steps, and under learner control, with attention cueing. Learner control is supported by the segmenting meta-analysis above; the evidence for self-paced animation is more mixed than for system-paced animation, because novices often do not use controls well.

Implication for lecture2audio. The content to be animated (algorithm execution, data-structure mutation, a derivation unfolding) is change over time, which is where animation is most defensible, and a step-through with a static final state shown at each step is functionally a sequence of static pictures, which avoids the transience problem. Animation of things that do not change (a definition, a static graph) is decoration and falls under the coherence principle.

## 4. Structure of good textbooks, worked examples and formal texts

**Direct evidence is thin.** Evidence on layout, glossaries, notation consistency and chunk sizes in textbooks is mostly indirect, drawn from the CLT and multimedia work above. Where I give a rule in section 8 on these points, its confidence is labelled weak or moderate accordingly.

**Definition-first versus example-first.** The relevant evidence is concreteness fading: Fyfe, McNeil, Son and Goldstone (2014) reviewed studies in which instruction starts with a concrete representation and then explicitly fades to the abstract, and recommend that order; they call it a systematic review, not a meta-analysis, and note that it is promising rather than settled. Educational Psychology Review, 26, 9-25. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3. Caveat for this project: the concrete representation in that literature is often a physical or pictorial one, and an analogy is a different thing. In our setting the concrete stage can be a concrete instance of the formal object (an actual array with numbers, an actual graph with labelled vertices), not an analogy from everyday life. The CLT literature also says novices benefit from seeing a worked example before solving, and a formal definition directly followed by a worked instance fits both.

**Analogies.** The user asked for none. The evidence is mixed in a way that supports this. Analogies can help, but benefit depends on cognitive supports: Richland, Zur and Holyoak (2007) found that the main difference between US and Hong Kong or Japanese mathematics lessons was not how often analogies were used but how much support was given for mapping source to target. Science, 316(5828), 1128-1129. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1142103 (found via search summaries; I did not read the full text). Without explicit mapping, the learner has to work out where the analogy breaks, which is a cost for a novice studying alone and a risk of carrying over wrong properties. I did not locate a clean meta-analysis of misconceptions induced by analogies, so the harm claim is theoretical. The best reading of the evidence is: analogies are not harmful by definition, but a text that has no teacher to explain the mapping and that wants exact definitions is better off without them, and a concrete instance of the actual object does the same job.

**Typography and layout.** Dyson and Haselgrove (2001) found that 55 characters per line gave better comprehension than 100, and was read faster than 25 or 100 (the speed claim is from citing papers). International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 54(4), 585-612. https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.2001.0458. Other line-length studies exist (found in results, not read); results are inconsistent on the exact optimum, and the screen-reading literature largely uses short passages. I did not find strong primary evidence on font size, line spacing, heading hierarchy or reading level for study material. These stay as conventions (weak).

**Signposting and organisers.** Signaling has a good evidence base (g = 0.53 retention, 0.33 transfer; Schneider et al., 2018), including explicit headings and a stated outline. Advance organizers are covered in section 5.

## 5. Prerequisites, knowledge components, organisers, concept maps

**Knowledge components.** Koedinger, Corbett and Perfetti (2012) propose that instruction should be designed around identified knowledge components (units of knowledge that can be learned and tested), with learning events chosen according to the type of component. Cognitive Science, 36(5), 757-798. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2012.01245.x. This is a framework, not a single test of an effect, but it underlies much educational data mining and gives a way to make "every idea is taught and tested" concrete. Their taxonomy also separates facts, which need memory support (retrieval practice), from principles and procedures, which need worked examples and practice with variation. This supports different check questions for definitions (recall) and for procedures (apply).

**Prerequisite order.** Pre-training (section 1) is the experimental support for teaching names and basic properties of components before the combined explanation. The CLT account of element interactivity predicts that a concept whose parts are not yet known is processed as many separate elements. I did not find a direct experiment that compares "terms always defined before use" against "terms defined at first use" for text; the rule is a design inference (moderate for the principle, weak for any specific threshold).

**Advance organizers.** I did not search for and verify a meta-analysis of advance organizers in this session, so I make no claim about their effect size. The outline-first and signaling findings above cover most of the same ground.

**Concept maps.** Nesbit and Adesope (2006) meta-analysed 55 studies (5,818 participants) and found that concept map study and construction increased retention, with effect size varying with how maps were used and the comparison treatment (Visible Learning lists about 0.55, secondary). Review of Educational Research, 76(3), 413-448. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543076003413. Against that, Karpicke and Blunt (2011) found retrieval practice beat concept-map construction. For this project the sensible use of a concept map is a per-course prerequisite graph, showing which definition depends on which, because it supports the term-ordering requirement; it should not replace check questions.

## 6. Self-explanation, generation, dual coding, learning styles

**Self-explanation.** Inducing self-explanation (prompting learners to explain steps to themselves) gave g = 0.55 over 69 effects. Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). Educational Psychology Review, 30(3), 703-725. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x. Note the tension with Barbieri et al. (2023), where self-explanation prompts moderated the worked-example effect negatively; one plausible reason (my inference, not tested) is that for complete novices prompts add load. Chi and Wylie's ICAP framework predicts that constructive activities (generating, explaining) beat active ones (highlighting), which beat passive ones, and that interactive (dialogue) beats constructive. Chi, M. T. H., & Wylie, R. (2014). Educational Psychologist, 49(4), 219-243. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1044018. ICAP is a framework with a good deal of supporting but mostly correlational or quasi-experimental evidence; treat it as a heuristic.

**Generation effect.** Generating information beats reading it, g about 0.40 over 445 effects in 86 studies, larger with longer retention intervals. Bertsch, S., Pesta, B. J., Wiscott, R., & McDaniel, M. A. (2007). Memory & Cognition, 35(2), 201-210. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441. Most generation tasks are word-level; the extension to complex concepts is weaker.

**Dual coding.** I did not verify a primary source on Paivio's dual coding theory in this session, so I make no quantitative claim. The multimedia and modality results above (graphics plus words beating words alone) are the practical support, and the redundancy result warns that duplicating the same content in two forms can hurt.

**Learning styles.** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008) found virtually no adequate evidence that matching instruction to a self-reported style (visual, verbal and so on) improves learning, since the needed interaction pattern was almost never tested and rarely found. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 9(3), 105-119. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x. The design consequence is that there is no reason to offer "visual mode" and "text mode" versions; offer one well-designed material in which every idea has its best representation.

## 7. Interactive documents and e-learning

**Active versus passive.** Freeman et al. (2014) meta-analysed 225 undergraduate STEM studies: active learning raised exam performance by 0.47 SD, and the odds of failing under lecturing were 1.95 times larger. PNAS, 111(23), 8410-8415. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1319030111. A published comment questioned the experimental design of the underlying studies. The "active" in these studies is mostly in-class problem solving and peer discussion, not clicking in a page, so this result cannot be transferred to interactive widgets without caution.

**Engagement is not learning.** Chi and Wylie's ICAP suggests that what matters is the cognitive activity, not overt interactivity: clicking "next" is active at best, and answering a question before seeing the answer is constructive. Tversky et al. (2002) make a parallel point about animation, where apparent benefits of interactive displays often came from added information. Alfieri et al. (2011) show that interactivity without guidance underperforms explicit instruction. The evidence therefore supports interaction that makes the learner retrieve, predict or explain, and does not support interaction for its own sake.

**Gaps in evidence.** I found no strong randomized evidence specific to HTML course pages with step-through visualisations versus static pages for novices in computer science. Most conclusions are extrapolated from laboratory multimedia studies. The project should therefore log (locally, if at all) which questions are answered wrongly and which controls are used, and treat the rules below as hypotheses to test.

## 8. Design rules for lecture2audio course material

Format of each rule: statement, rationale, source, confidence. Numeric thresholds are starting values derived by inference from the cited sources (mostly Cowan 2001 and CLT); they are not experimentally established thresholds and should be tuned.

### Chunking and cognitive load

1. **Limit new elements per chunk.** A chunk (one subsection under one heading) introduces at most four new terms, symbols or rules that interact with each other, because novice working memory holds about three to five chunks. If a topic needs more, split it into subsections and order them so that each subsection depends only on earlier ones. Sources: Cowan (2001); Sweller (2010). Confidence: moderate for the principle, weak for the number four.
2. **Keep subsections short.** A subsection should present one idea in at most about 150 words of prose plus its definition, formula and one visual. If it is longer, split it. Sources: segmenting (Rey et al., 2019) and the CLT argument; the number is a convention. Confidence: moderate for segmenting, weak for the count.
3. **Remove everything that does not serve the stated objective.** No decorative images, anecdotes or tangents. A fact stays only if it is taught in the lecture, is needed to understand a lecture item, or is a marked external gap-filler. Sources: Rey (2012); Mayer and Fiorella (coherence). Confidence: strong for the direction, moderate for size.
4. **No duplicated content in two forms in one place.** Do not repeat the same sentence as running text and as a caption. A caption should add what the figure cannot show on its own. Source: redundancy principle (Mayer and Fiorella; the effect size was revised downward in the latest edition). Confidence: moderate.

### Order of presentation

5. **Define before use, in prerequisite order.** Every term and symbol is defined before the first sentence that uses it, within the same lecture or in an earlier lecture of the same course, with a link back to the definition. Sources: pre-training (Mayer et al., 2002); Koedinger et al. (2012); CLT. Confidence: moderate.
6. **Give an overview first.** Each lecture page begins with a list of the section titles and the terms that will be defined, in the order of the page, and each section starts with a one-sentence statement of what will be learned. Sources: signaling (Schneider et al., 2018); pre-training. Confidence: moderate.
7. **Order inside an idea: formal definition, then one concrete instance of the same object, then the general statement or procedure, then a check.** The concrete instance is an actual instance of the formal object (for example the array [3, 1, 2] with the operation applied), not an analogy. Sources: Fyfe et al. (2014) for concrete-to-abstract fading, adapted; Barbieri et al. (2023) for worked examples. Confidence: moderate. Note that concreteness fading itself proposes concrete before abstract, whereas here the formal statement comes first because the reader needs exact definitions; the order within an idea is not settled by the literature and is worth an A/B test.
8. **Worked examples before practice.** The first time a procedure appears, show a fully worked example with every step, and only then ask the reader to do a similar one. Sources: Barbieri et al. (2023); Sweller's worked-example effect. Confidence: strong for novices.
9. **Fade the worked example.** After the first complete example, later examples of the same procedure may omit the last step or two for the reader to complete. Sources: worked-example fading in CLT work (not independently verified in this session) and the expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga et al., 2003). Confidence: weak.
10. **No analogies or metaphors.** Explain using the formal object and concrete instances of it. Sources: Richland et al. (2007) show analogies depend on explicit mapping support; no direct evidence of harm was found. Confidence: weak (this rule is a requirement of the user, supported by the argument rather than by an experiment).

### Visuals and animation

11. **Show a visual when the idea involves structure or change over time**: data structures, graph or tree shape, algorithm state, matrices, functions. Do not show a visual for a definition that is purely verbal. Sources: Höffler and Leutner (2007) on representational versus decorative; Rey (2012). Confidence: moderate.
12. **Place labels in the visual, next to what they label, or number the elements and refer to them by number in the nearby text.** No label in the text that requires searching the figure. Sources: Chandler and Sweller (1991); spatial contiguity (Mayer and Fiorella). Confidence: strong.
13. **Animations are step-through by default, not autoplay.** Each step is one change (one comparison, one pointer move, one swap), with Previous and Next controls and a visible step counter, and an optional play mode that is slow. After each step the full state is visible, so the reader never has to remember the previous frame. Sources: Tversky et al. (2002); Hegarty (2004); Rey et al. (2019) for segmenting; Berney and Bétrancourt (2016) (system-paced gave a larger effect, which I treat as a reason to give a good default pace and not to prefer autoplay). Confidence: moderate.
14. **Signal what changed in each step**: highlight the changed element and the code line being executed, in a consistent colour, with a one-sentence caption of the step in the same place. Sources: signaling (Schneider et al., 2018); the cueing recommendation of the animation literature. Confidence: moderate to strong.
15. **One idea per animation, at most about eight steps.** Longer processes are split into separate animations. Sources: segmenting; the limit of the number is my inference. Confidence: weak.
16. **Provide a static summary beside each animation**: the final state or a small table of states, so the reader can review without replaying. Sources: Tversky et al. (2002) on transience; Berney and Bétrancourt (2016). Confidence: weak.
17. **Ask for a prediction before a step, sometimes.** For an algorithm, offer an optional "what happens next?" input before revealing a step, with immediate feedback. Sources: pretesting (Richland et al., 2009), ICAP. Confidence: weak for this use (evidence is about text and recall tasks).
18. **No audio, no autoplay motion on page load, and respect the system's reduced-motion setting.** Sources: coherence; accessibility convention, not a learning-science result. Confidence: weak.

### Retrieval, spacing, feedback

19. **A check question follows every concept subsection.** Each defined term and each procedure has at least one question: recall of the definition, application to a new instance, or a trace of the procedure. The answer and a short worked explanation are shown after the reader commits (typed, chosen, or by a "reveal" gesture after the reader has formed an answer). Sources: Rowland (2014); Roediger and Karpicke (2006); Pan and Rickard (2018) (untested neighbours do not benefit); Hattie and Timperley (2007). Confidence: strong.
20. **Questions are hidden answers by default.** The answer is collapsed until the reader asks for it. Source: the testing and generation effects (Bertsch et al., 2007). Confidence: strong for retrieval, weak for the specific interface.
21. **Scaffolds can be collapsed.** Offer a control to collapse worked-out steps, definitions repeated from earlier lectures, and prerequisite reminders for returning readers; show them expanded for the first visit. Sources: expertise reversal (Kalyuga et al., 2003). Confidence: moderate.
22. **Spaced review.** Each lecture page ends with a short review set containing questions from earlier lectures of the same course (at least one from each of the previous two lectures, where they exist), and these questions are placed also in a course-level review page. Sources: Cepeda et al. (2006); Dunlosky et al. (2013). Confidence: strong for spacing in general, moderate for this implementation.
23. **Interleave in review sets.** In review sets, mix question types from different topics, and especially problems that look alike but need different procedures. Do not interleave when a concept is first taught. Sources: Brunmair and Richter (2019); Rohrer et al. (2020). Confidence: moderate.
24. **Do not rely on highlighting, summaries or "key point" boxes as the learning activity.** A summary box is allowed only as a recall aid after the check, and should not replace the questions. Source: Dunlosky et al. (2013). Confidence: moderate.
25. **No unassisted discovery tasks.** Any exploratory widget must tell the reader what to look for and give feedback. Source: Alfieri et al. (2011). Confidence: moderate.

### Notation, terminology and language

26. **One term, one symbol, one definition, course-wide.** Use the termbase for the whole course. A symbol never changes meaning between lectures; if the lecture used two names for one thing, the page uses the first defined name and lists the other as an alias in the glossary. Sources: element interactivity (Sweller, 2010): inconsistent notation multiplies the elements a novice must track; Koedinger et al. (2012). No direct experiment on notation consistency in textbooks was found. Confidence: weak to moderate (strong in practical terms, weak as evidence).
27. **Mark the defining sentence visibly and make it exact.** A definition is a standalone block with a fixed label ("Definition"), the term in bold at the point of definition, the exact formal statement, and the notation. Sources: signaling (Schneider et al., 2018). Confidence: moderate for signaling, weak for the exact format.
28. **Link every use of a term to its definition and provide a course glossary.** On hover or click the definition appears; a glossary page lists every term, symbol, and the lecture of first definition. Sources: pre-training; split attention (the definition is integrated at the point of use, not on another page). Confidence: weak to moderate.
29. **Plain, direct sentences.** One claim per sentence, active voice where possible, no undefined idioms, at most about 20 words per sentence on average, no more than 30 for any one sentence, and no more than two clauses. Sources: CLT in general (element interactivity); I found no primary study that tests a specific sentence length for study text. Confidence: weak (convention).
30. **Mark external material.** Any statement that is not in the lecture is visibly marked as an addition with its source, as already done in script.md. Source: coherence principle and trust; no learning-science source for the marker itself. Confidence: weak.

### Layout and typography

31. **Line length of about 45 to 75 characters for running text.** Source: Dyson and Haselgrove (2001), which compared 25, 55 and 100 characters. Confidence: moderate for the claim that very long lines are worse, weak for the exact range.
32. **Visible hierarchy of three levels at most**, in step with the existing part, point and sub-point structure of the pipeline: lecture title, section, subsection. Sources: signaling; CLT. Confidence: weak.
33. **One column of content, with the visual placed right after the sentence that introduces it.** Sources: spatial contiguity; Chandler and Sweller (1991). Confidence: strong for contiguity, weak for the exact layout.
34. **Every formula is typeset with the same notation as its definition and has each symbol explained once, on the first use in the section.** Source: Sweller (2010). Confidence: weak.

### What to hide by default

35. **Hidden until asked:** answers to check questions, optional derivations or proofs that were not part of the lecture, the long list of alternative names, and any marked external digression. **Visible by default:** definitions, the worked example, the visual and its caption, the section overview. Sources: coherence (Rey, 2012); retrieval (Rowland, 2014). Confidence: moderate.

## 9. Rules code can check automatically

These can become gates in the same style as the existing gates in the pipeline (word counts, term-before-use, and so on). All are checks on the structure the generator produces, so they should run on the intermediate JSON before HTML is rendered.

| Rule | Check | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Count new terms and symbols introduced per subsection (from the termbase entries whose first use is in this subsection) and fail above four. | hard gate, tunable threshold |
| 2 | Word count of prose per subsection, excluding definition, code and formula blocks; warn above 150, fail above 250. | gate |
| 3, 35 | Flag any block without a type from the schema (definition, explanation, example, question, visual, external). Check that every sentence traces to a lecture unit or to an external source id. This extends the existing coverage and `check_facts` gates. | hard gate |
| 4 | Detect near-duplicate sentences between caption and prose in the same subsection (normalised string similarity). | warn |
| 5, 26, 28 | Term-before-use: for each term in the termbase, its first occurrence in page order must be its definition block, or an earlier lecture's definition. The existing `stray_terms` and "term used before its part" checks already do this for scripts. Also check that one symbol maps to one term across the course. | hard gate |
| 6 | A page must begin with an overview listing the section titles that follow, and each section begins with a one-sentence objective. | hard gate |
| 7, 8 | Each procedure or definition subsection has the block sequence definition, instance, check (order check on the block types); each procedure has at least one worked example before its first practice question. | hard gate |
| 11, 13, 14, 15 | Every visual declares its type; every animation has controls (previous, next, step counter), at most eight steps, and a caption and highlight target for each step; no autoplay on load. Check the schema, not the pixels. | hard gate |
| 12 | Every label in a visual's spec is attached to an element id; every figure referenced in text is within the same subsection. | gate |
| 19, 20 | Every definition and every procedure has at least one question with a hidden answer; every question has an answer and an explanation. Also a coverage count: number of tested terms divided by defined terms must be 1.0. | hard gate |
| 22, 23 | Review set exists and contains at least one question from each of the two previous lectures when they exist. | hard gate |
| 29 | Sentence length (mean and maximum), number of clauses (approximate by commas and conjunctions), and passive voice detector; warn only, because these heuristics are unreliable. | warn |
| 30 | Every external-source sentence has a source id verified by `check_facts`; it appears in the reference list. | hard gate (exists) |
| 31, 33 | CSS check: content column `max-width` between 45ch and 75ch. | gate |
| 32 | Heading depth at most three. | gate (like the existing outline rule) |
| 34 | Every symbol appearing in a formula appears in the termbase symbol table of the course. | gate |

Rules that a code gate cannot decide, and that need the model judge or human review: whether an example is a real instance of the defined object (not an analogy; rule 10, which a judge can check by prompt and which should be audited by a planted-analogy test in the same spirit as the planted-omission test), whether a visual shows the right mechanism (rule 11), whether a question tests the idea it claims to test (rule 19), whether a sentence is plain (rule 29), and the order of definition and example (rule 7, which needs an A/B test with real readers).

Rules which should never be enforced by hard gates because the evidence is weak: numeric thresholds in rules 1, 2, 15 and 29. Enforce them as warnings at first, and calibrate on lectures where students report difficulty.
